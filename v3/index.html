You have a goal. You know exactly what it needs. And somehow, you keep not doing it.

03 · what your brain is actually doing

A smart brain, doing an old job

Here’s the honest version, without the drama: your brain was built for a different problem. It was built to keep you alive — to prefer certain over uncertain, familiar over new, comfort over cost. A goal is the opposite of all three. It asks you to trade a sure now for a maybe later. So your brain rarely argues with the goal. It does something quieter: it keeps your hands busy with anything else.

The tricky part — the part that keeps thoughtful people convinced they’re just “curious” or “being thorough” — is that the distractions aren’t fake. The interest is real. That’s the camouflage. Your brain picks escapes you genuinely enjoy, because escapes you enjoy are the hardest to notice. That’s why an entire evening can disappear while you stay busy, and why “I was doing something related” is the truest-sounding lie you will ever tell yourself.

So here is the pattern, said plainly: every time you get close to the uncomfortable core of the goal, something interesting flares up. The timing is not an accident. It’s protection — your brain steering you back into a loop it can predict. The loop isn’t a character flaw. It’s a habit with a job description.

And here’s the part people miss: noticing the flare-up — in the moment, while it’s happening — is not a small thing. It is the first real move of the whole game. You can’t out-argue the loop, but you can see it coming. And once you can see it, the exit is always the same modest door: ten minutes of the real thing, badly begun.

You can’t out-argue the loop. But you can see it coming — and seeing it is already the first step out of it.
